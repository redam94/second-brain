# Case Study: Fat Arms and Political Attitudes

From [[Gelman and Loken 2013 - The Garden of Forking Paths]], Section 2.1.

## Original Study

Petersen et al. (2013) claimed to find an association between men's upper-body strength (measured via arm circumference), socioeconomic status, and attitudes about economic redistribution.

## Forking Paths Identified

### Measurement Issues
- Two of three studies used college students, not general population
- Arm circumference was used as a proxy for upper-body strength
- The proxy nature of these measures was not made transparent

### Main Effects vs. Interactions
- The authors reported a statistically significant *interaction* with no significant main effect
- Had they found a main effect, that would have been equally publishable
- Had neither appeared, other interactions (e.g., comparing students with/without older siblings) could have been explored

### Confounding
- Arm circumference in young men is likely a proxy for age
- When age is controlled for, the coefficient for arm circumference disappears
- The interaction between arm circumference and SES does not adjust for the interaction between age and SES
- Political attitudes change around college age, creating a plausible confound

## Lesson

A single overarching research hypothesis can correspond to many different statistical tests. The choice among them is a [[Researcher Degrees of Freedom]] problem even when the researcher performs only one test.
