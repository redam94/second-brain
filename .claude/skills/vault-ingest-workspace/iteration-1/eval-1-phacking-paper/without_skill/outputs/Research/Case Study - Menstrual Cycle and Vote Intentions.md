# Case Study: Menstrual Cycle and Vote Intentions

From [[Gelman and Loken 2013 - The Garden of Forking Paths]], Section 2.3.

## Original Study

Durante, Arsena, and Griskevicius (2013), published in *Psychological Science*, claimed that ovulation had different effects on single versus married women's political preferences, religiosity, and voting intentions.

## Forking Paths Identified

### Study Design Issues
- The study was entirely between-subjects (each woman surveyed once)
- Correlations were found between stated preferences and menstrual cycle day
- No repeated measurements -- causal language in the paper was misleading

### Flexibility in Analysis Choices
- **Main effects vs. interactions:** The authors focused on the interaction between ovulation and relationship status as their central result
- **Multiple outcome measures:** Attitudes, vote intention, religiosity -- any of which could have been the highlighted finding
- **Demographic flexibility:** Age, ethnicity, parenthood status could serve as moderators
- **Relationship categorization:** "Single" vs. "married" was defined flexibly -- engaged, living with partner, or married counted as "committed"

### Implausible Effect Size
- The claimed effect was approximately a 20 percentage point difference in vote intention across menstrual cycle phases
- This is substantively implausible given that very few people change vote intentions during presidential campaigns
- The study had very low power, making any observed difference likely attributable to noise

## Lesson

The combinatorial explosion of possible analyses (outcomes x subgroups x interaction terms x demographic controls) creates an enormous [[Garden of Forking Paths]], even when each choice flows naturally from theory. The researchers would not have felt they were making choices at all.
