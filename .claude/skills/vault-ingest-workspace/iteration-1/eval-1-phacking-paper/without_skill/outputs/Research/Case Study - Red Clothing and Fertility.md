# Case Study: Red Clothing and Fertility

From [[Gelman and Loken 2013 - The Garden of Forking Paths]], Sections 3.0--3.2. This is the most detailed case study in the paper.

## Original Study

Beall and Tracy (2013), published in *Psychological Science*, reported that women were three times more likely to wear red or pink shirts at peak fertility compared to other points in their menstrual cycles.

## Forking Paths Identified

### Data Exclusion Rules (Section 3.1)
- 9 of 24 women in the second sample did not meet the stated inclusion criterion (more than 5 days from menses onset) but were included anyway
- 22% of the first sample also failed the criterion
- The first sample was supposed to be restricted to women under 40, but included women up to age 47
- 31% of participants were excluded for "insufficient precision" -- but precision of self-reported cycle timing varies with time since menses

### Color Coding Choices
- The significant pattern appeared for red and pink combined
- Had the pattern appeared only for red, or only for pink, the theory would still have supported it
- Had white or gray been the significant colors, a story about "bland colors highlighting pinkish skin tones" could have been constructed

### Cycle Day Boundaries
- "Peak fertility" was defined as days 6--14 of a 28-day cycle
- Medical sources disagree: womenshealth.gov says days 10--17; babycenter.com says days 12--17
- The comparison group (days outside 6--14) was also a choice -- other day ranges could have been used
- Compare with Durante et al. (2013), which used days 7--14 vs. days 17--25 (excluding many other days entirely)

### Combining Information Across Studies (Section 3.2)
- Beall and Tracy found consistent main effects across two samples (internet adults and college students)
- Durante et al., using similar methodology on a similar topic, found no main effect but a significant interaction
- Either approach (pooling for main effects or splitting for interactions) would have been defensible

## The Stark Comparison

Beall and Tracy and Durante et al. published in the same journal in the same year on similar topics with similar methods, but made different data-analytic choices, each finding statistical significance. This illustrates the garden of forking paths vividly: whichever route you take seems predetermined.

## Lesson

Data exclusion rules, variable coding, and analytic boundaries that feel like the only natural choice are often contingent on the data. Different data could have led to different but equally reasonable choices, each potentially producing statistical significance.
