# Blake Nosko Tadelis 2015 - Paid Search Effectiveness - Overview

> [!summary]
> Blake, Nosko & Tadelis report a series of eBay field experiments that switched paid search (SEM) off. **Brand-keyword** ads ("ebay shoes") had no measurable short-term value: when they were paused on MSN, 99.5% of the lost click traffic came back through the free organic link. For **non-brand** keywords, eBay stopped bidding in 68 of 210 US DMAs for about 60 days. The difference-in-differences effect of the whole paid search programme on sales was +0.66% (s.e. 0.55%, not significant). Converted into return on investment this is **−63%** (95% CI −124% to −3%), against **+4,173%** from a naive OLS regression of log revenue on log spend and +1,632% with DMA and day fixed effects. The average hides heterogeneity: ads moved new registrants and customers who bought only 1-2 times in the prior year, but most paid search spend went on frequent buyers who would have bought anyway. The authors read this as support for the **informative view** of advertising.
^bnt-summary

## Overview

**Bibliographic and context details (title and abstract pages).**
- Thomas (Tom) Blake, Chris Nosko and Steven Tadelis, "Consumer Heterogeneity and Paid Search Effectiveness: A Large Scale Field Experiment". **NBER Working Paper No. 20171, May 2014.** Published in *Econometrica* 83(1):155-174, January 2015 (DOI 10.3982/ECTA12423), with an online supplement holding most regression tables.
- **JEL codes:** C93 (field experiments), D22 (firm behaviour, empirical), L10, L20, L81 (retail and wholesale trade; e-commerce), M37 (advertising).
- **Affiliations (WP):** Blake, eBay Research Labs. Nosko, Booth School of Business, University of Chicago, and eBay Research Labs. Tadelis, Haas School of Business, UC Berkeley, and NBER.
- **Data access and interests.** The WP title page states: "This work was done while Tadelis and Nosko were employed by ebay Research Labs." All three authors worked inside the advertiser whose campaigns were tested. That gave them access to eBay's internal sales and click data and to the experiments themselves. Note the direction of the potential interest: the finding that the advertiser's *own* spend was wasted runs against the ad platform, not against the employer, and the experiments led eBay to change strategy (as Lewis & Rao 2015 report, see [[Lewis Rao 2015 - Robustness, Scale, Targeting and Measurement Technology]]). The same access is why DMA-level revenue and cost are withheld, and why public figures are used for the ROI (§5).
- Differences between the WP (used for these notes) and the journal version: [[Blake 2015 - Working Paper vs Econometrica Version Differences]]. All estimates are identical, but tables and equations are renumbered and some Discussion passages appear only in the WP.

**Research question.** What is the causal short-term return on paid search for a large, well-known advertiser, and why do standard industry methods get it so wrong?

**Contribution.**
1. A clean statement of a form of endogeneity specific to pay-per-click media. Spend is *generated by* consumer intent, since the advertiser pays only when someone clicks, so spend and sales are correlated even with zero causal effect ([[Blake 2015 - SEM Mechanics and the Endogeneity of Click-Priced Spend]]).
2. Large-scale experimental evidence. Platform-level shutdowns of brand keywords and a geo-randomized shutdown of all non-brand keywords together produce ROI estimates two orders of magnitude below observational ones.
3. A heterogeneity analysis by purchase **frequency** and **recency** showing *who* responds, plus a null result across 378 product categories.
4. A simple formula that turns a log-sales DiD coefficient into ROI ($\text{ROI}\approx\frac{\beta_1}{1+\beta_1}\frac{R_1}{S_1}-1$), and a way to put OLS and IV elasticities on the same scale.

**One-line takeaway.** For an advertiser that consumers already know, click-priced ads mostly buy the clicks of people who were coming anyway. Attribution and spend-on-sales regressions credit those clicks to the ads, and only a randomized holdout separates the two.

## Examples

> [!example] Reading the paper as a marketing analyst (worked example)
> A retailer's attribution dashboard credits paid search with \$10M of sales on \$1M of spend, a "ROAS of 10". The paper's logic gives three checks:
> 1. **Is the term navigational?** For brand terms, assume near-complete organic substitution (§3) until a holdout shows otherwise.
> 2. **Who is clicking?** If most attributed buyers are frequent customers (the Fig. 5 pattern), the incremental share is small.
> 3. **Run the geo holdout.** With a DiD log-sales effect $\beta_1$ and $R_1/S_1$ the ratio of total revenue to search spend, $\text{ROI}\approx\frac{\beta_1}{1+\beta_1}\frac{R_1}{S_1}-1$. Example: $\beta_1=0.01$, revenue \$500M, spend \$5M gives $\frac{0.01}{1.01}\times100-1=-1\%$. The channel roughly breaks even, whatever the dashboard says.
> See [[Blake 2015 - ROI Derivation from Experimental Log-Sales Effects]].

## Connections

- **Geo-experiment family.** The non-brand test is an early, large-scale geo experiment that analyses matched DMAs with DiD. It sits next to [[Geo-Experiment Methodology - Overview]], [[Geo-Experiment Design and Power Analysis]] and [[Time-Based Regression Estimator for Geo Experiments]]. The paper cites Vaver & Koehler (2011) for the method.
- **ITT logic.** Turning ads off in a region is an intent-to-treat design, as noted in [[Intent-to-Treat, PSA and Ghost Ad Designs]]. Misclassification of user location dilutes it towards zero (fn. 20).
- **Observational failure.** It belongs with [[Activity Bias in Advertising]], [[Observational vs Experimental Methods in Advertising]] and [[Experimental Benchmarks for Observational Ad Measurement]]: correlational ROI overstated by one to two orders of magnitude.
- **Sibling papers in this cluster.** [[Lewis Rao 2015 - Unfavorable Economics of Ad Measurement - Overview]] is the published form of the Lewis & Rao (2013) power critique cited in fn. 12, and it treats this experiment as the exception that changed an advertiser's strategy ([[Lewis Rao 2015 - Robustness, Scale, Targeting and Measurement Technology]]). Its impact-to-SD derivation ([[Lewis Rao 2015 - Impact-to-SD Ratio Derivation and Calibrated Campaign]]) explains why even this huge test leaves a ±60-point ROI interval. [[Gordon et al 2019 - Facebook RCTs vs Observational Ad Measurement - Overview]] positions its user-level RCTs against this paper's geo-level DiD ([[Gordon et al 2019 - Conclusions, Caveats and Related Literature]], [[Gordon et al 2019 - Facebook Experiment Design and Exposure Endogeneity]]).
- **Power.** The −124% to −3% interval on a \$51M channel illustrates [[The Unfavorable Economics of Ad Experiments - Power and Signal-to-Noise]].
- **MMM.** Paid search is the textbook selection-bias channel in [[Chan Perry 2017 - Challenges in Media Mix Modeling - Overview]], and the [[Zhang Vaver 2017 - Aggregate Marketing System Simulator - Overview|AMSS simulator]] builds paid-versus-organic query substitution into its ground truth. Experimental ROI like this is what [[Q - Using Experiment Results as Priors in a Bayesian MMM]] calibrates against.
- **Proxy metrics.** Attributed sales are a proxy that diverges from incremental sales under optimization pressure (see [[Manheim Garrabrant 2018 - Variants of Goodhart's Law - Overview]]).
