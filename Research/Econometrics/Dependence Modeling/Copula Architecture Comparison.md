# Copula Architecture Comparison

> [!summary]
> The copula modelling literature has converged on four main architectural families for high-dimensional dependence: **elliptical copulas** (Gaussian, Student-$t$), **Archimedean copulas** (Clayton, Gumbel, Frank), **vine copulas** (C-vine, D-vine, R-vine), and **factor copulas** (Oh & Patton). Each family occupies a distinct position in the flexibility–parsimony–scalability space. This note provides a structured comparison: which architecture to choose for a given application, and the mathematical properties that determine that choice.

## Overview

Choosing a copula architecture involves trade-offs across five dimensions:

1. **Scalability** — how large can $n$ be before the model breaks down or becomes computationally infeasible?
2. **Flexibility** — can the model capture asymmetric and heterogeneous dependence, or is it constrained to symmetric or equidependent structures?
3. **Parameter count** — how many parameters grow with $n$? Fewer parameters → easier estimation, less overfitting risk, more interpretable.
4. **Tail dependence** — can the model capture correlated crashes (lower tail) or correlated booms (upper tail)?
5. **Estimation method** — closed-form likelihood (fast MLE) vs. simulation-based (slower but more flexible).

### Elliptical Copulas

> [!definition] Gaussian and Student-$t$ Copulas
> Derived from multivariate normal and Student-$t$ distributions via Sklar's theorem. The correlation matrix $\Sigma$ (normalised to a correlation matrix $R$) fully determines the copula structure.
>
> **Gaussian copula:**
> $$C_\Sigma^{\text{Ga}}(u_1,\ldots,u_n) = \Phi_\Sigma(\Phi^{-1}(u_1),\ldots,\Phi^{-1}(u_n))$$
> **Properties:** Zero tail dependence (asymptotically independent in both tails — the crisis-era failure mode); symmetric upper/lower structure; easy to scale to $n=1000$ via correlation-shrinkage or factor structure for $\Sigma$; fast closed-form MLE.
>
> **Student-$t$ copula:**
> $$C_{\Sigma,\nu}^t(u_1,\ldots,u_n) = t_{\Sigma,\nu}(t_\nu^{-1}(u_1),\ldots,t_\nu^{-1}(u_n))$$
> **Properties:** Positive and symmetric tail dependence (crashes and booms equally correlated); same upper and lower tail dependence coefficient $\tau = 2t_{\nu+1}(-\sqrt{(\nu+1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)})$; symmetric around zero; impossible to have asymmetric crisis vs. boom dependence. For equity returns, the symmetric tail assumption is strongly rejected (Oh & Patton 2012, Table 7).
>
> **Practical limitation:** Estimating $n(n-1)/2$ correlations in high dimension requires regularisation (factor structure or shrinkage). The standard $t$-copula has a single degree-of-freedom parameter shared across all pairs.
^def-elliptical

### Archimedean Copulas

> [!definition] Archimedean Copulas
> Generated by a **generator function** $\phi:[0,\infty) \to [0,1]$ with $\phi(0)=1$, $\phi(\infty)=0$, $(-1)^k\phi^{(k)}\geq 0$:
> $$C(u_1,\ldots,u_n;\theta) = \phi\!\left(\phi^{-1}(u_1)+\cdots+\phi^{-1}(u_n)\right)$$
>
> | Family | Generator $\phi(t;\theta)$ | Tail dep. | Range of $\theta$ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Clayton | $(1+t)^{-1/\theta}$ | Lower (upper = 0) | $\theta > 0$ |
> | Gumbel | $\exp(-t^{1/\theta})$ | Upper (lower = 0) | $\theta \geq 1$ |
> | Frank | $-\log\frac{1-e^{-\theta t}}{1-e^{-\theta}}$ | None | $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ |
>
> **Key limitation for $n > 2$:** All pairs share the **same** bivariate copula (exchangeability). Every pair $(X_i, X_j)$ has the identical bivariate copula — the model cannot capture heterogeneous dependence across pairs. This is almost never true in financial or marketing applications. Hierarchical Archimedean copulas partially address this but introduce model selection complexity.
>
> **Advantage:** One parameter scales trivially to any dimension. Useful as pair-copula components within a vine.
^def-archimedean

### Vine Copulas

> [!definition] Vine Copula Strengths and Weaknesses
> **Strengths:**
> - Maximum flexibility: each of $\binom{n}{2}$ pair copulas can be from a different bivariate family
> - Can capture asymmetric tail dependence in any direction for any pair
> - Interpretable at the pair level; tree structure has a graphical interpretation
> - Sequential estimation is computationally feasible and well-understood
>
> **Weaknesses:**
> - $\binom{n}{2}$ parameters grows **quadratically** with $n$: for $n=100$, there are 4950 pair copulas to estimate — each requiring 1–2 parameters and a family selection. This is computationally and statistically infeasible without strong truncation
> - **Structure selection** in general R-vines requires heuristic algorithms (no polynomial-time exact solution for large $n$)
> - **Interpretability decays** rapidly with tree level: a tree-5 conditional copula conditioning on 4 variables is hard to assign economic meaning
> - The **simplifying assumption** (constant conditional copulas) is often violated and hard to test; inference breaks down when it fails
> - High-tree conditional pseudo-observations accumulate estimation error from lower-tree $h$-functions
>
> **Practical range:** Vine copulas are widely used for $n = 3$ to $30$. Above $n \approx 30$, truncated vines (using only trees 1–3) or factor copulas are preferred.
^def-vine-strengths

### Factor Copulas

> [!definition] Factor Copula Strengths and Weaknesses
> (From [[Factor Copulas - Overview]] and [[Factor Copula Construction]]; summarised here for comparison.)
>
> **Strengths:**
> - Extreme **parsimony**: the equidependence model has 2–4 parameters regardless of $n$; the block model has $\sim K^2 + n$ parameters for $K$ industry groups
> - **Scales to $n=100$+**: all 100 S&P 100 constituents estimated in Oh & Patton (2012)
> - **Interpretable**: the latent factor corresponds to a market/macro risk driver; tail dependence has analytical EVT derivations (see [[Tail Dependence in Factor Copulas]])
> - Can capture fat-tailed AND asymmetric dependence simultaneously via skew-$t$ common factor
> - Analytical tail-dependence coefficients from EVT (not just numerical simulation)
>
> **Weaknesses:**
> - Imposes a **structural restriction**: dependence is driven by a latent factor, not directly specified pair-by-pair
> - The latent factor structure may be rejected by the data in some applications
> - Estimation requires **SMM** (no closed-form likelihood): slower, requires careful moment selection
> - Heterogeneous pairwise dependence requires the block model with many groups or the flexible-weight model with $n-1$ additional loading parameters
>
> **When factor copulas beat vine copulas:**
> - $n > 30$ (vine becomes infeasible or must be severely truncated)
> - Economic theory supports a factor structure (market risk driving all equity dependence)
> - Need for interpretable systemic risk measures (MES, $kES$) with analytical properties
>
> **When vine copulas beat factor copulas:**
> - $n \leq 20$ with highly heterogeneous pairwise dependence structures
> - Time series with a natural ordering (D-vine captures chain dependence)
> - No strong prior for a factor structure; pair-by-pair flexibility is needed
^def-factor-strengths

### Head-to-Head: Factor vs. Vine

> [!example] Oh & Patton (2012) Model Comparison (Table 6)
> **Setting:** 100 S&P 100 equity returns, 2008–2010. 18 copula models compared by out-of-sample likelihood and goodness of fit.
>
> **Key finding (rephrased from Oh & Patton 2012, Sec. 4.3):** The paper explicitly compares against vine copulas (Aas et al. 2009) and concludes that vine copulas are **"hard-to-interpret/test assumptions"** in very high dimensions and become infeasible without truncation. The skew-$t$–$t$ block factor copula (16 parameters for 100 variables) significantly outperforms the Gaussian copula and Student-$t$ copula in both in-sample fit and out-of-sample predictive likelihood.
>
> **Practical implication:** For $n = 100$, even a severely truncated vine (trees 1-2 only, independence copula for trees 3+) would use $n-1 + n-2 = 197$ pair copulas — still far more parameters than the 16-parameter block factor copula, with worse interpretability.

## When to Use Which Architecture

> [!definition] Decision Guide
>
> | Application | Recommended architecture | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | $n \leq 5$, flexible dependence | **Vine copula** | Few pairs; full flexibility; easy to interpret all trees |
> | $5 < n \leq 20$, heterogeneous pairs | **Vine copula** (truncated at tree 2–4) | Captures strongest pair dependence; higher trees often near independence |
> | $n > 30$, factor structure plausible | **Factor copula** | Parsimony at scale; analytically tractable tail dependence |
> | $n > 30$, no factor structure | **Factor copula + block** or **GARCH-DCC** | Block factor captures group dependence; DCC extends dynamic correlation |
> | Time series chain dependence | **D-vine** | Natural ordering maps to tree 1 neighbours; Markov-vine as special case |
> | One dominant variable | **C-vine** | Root variable captures all direct dependencies in tree 1 |
> | Symmetric dependence, large $n$ | **Gaussian or Student-$t$ copula** | Scalable; many correlation regularisation methods available |
> | One-sided tail dependence | **Archimedean** or **vine with Clayton/Gumbel pair** | Single-parameter parsimonious; use as pair copula within vine |
