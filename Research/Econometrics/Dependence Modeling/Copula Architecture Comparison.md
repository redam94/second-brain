# Copula Architecture Comparison

> [!summary]
> Four copula architectures dominate applied work: **elliptical** (Gaussian, $t$), **Archimedean** (Clayton, Gumbel, Frank), **vine** (C-vine, D-vine, R-vine), and **factor copulas**. Each makes different choices on tail dependence, asymmetry, parameter count, and scalability. This note organises these trade-offs to guide architecture selection for a given application and dimension.

## Overview

Sklar's theorem guarantees that any joint distribution has a copula, but practitioners must choose a copula *model* from a tractable parametric family. No single architecture dominates:  elliptical copulas are interpretable but constrained; Archimedean copulas are parsimonious but exchangeable; vine copulas are flexible but high-dimensional; factor copulas are parsimonious but impose a global structure. The choice depends on: dimension $d$, the nature of tail dependence (symmetric/asymmetric), data size $T$, and interpretability requirements.

## Main Content

> [!definition] Architecture taxonomy
>
> | Architecture | Examples | Key property | Tail dependence | Asymmetry | Parameters | Scalability |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | **Elliptical** | Gaussian, Student's $t$ | Defined by covariance/correlation matrix $\Sigma$ | Gaussian: zero; $t$: symmetric positive | None | $d(d-1)/2$ correlations | $O(d^2)$; feasible to $d\approx 100$ with shrinkage |
> | **Archimedean** | Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, Joe | Generated by a single univariate function $\phi$ (generator) | Clayton: lower; Gumbel: upper; Frank: zero; Joe: upper | Clayton/Gumbel/Joe: directional; Frank: symmetric | 1–2 parameters | $O(1)$; trivially scalable but *exchangeable* (all pairs identical) |
> | **Vine** | C-vine, D-vine, R-vine | Hierarchical pair-copula construction | Flexible: each pair can have its own family | Flexible: rotated copulas allow any direction | $d(d-1)/2$ pair copulas × 1-2 params each | Feasible for $d \leq 20$–30; truncation needed for larger $d$ |
> | **Factor** | Simple/equidependent, block, skew-$t$ factor | Latent factor $X_i = \beta_i Z + \varepsilon_i$ | Controlled globally by factor tail (skew $t$: asymmetric, positive) | Yes if $F_z$ skewed | 2–16 params for $N=100$ | $O(N)$; explicitly designed for $d > 50$ |
^def-taxonomy

### Detailed comparisons

> [!definition] Tail dependence comparison
> **Tail dependence coefficient** $\lambda^U = \lim_{q\to 1} P(X_j > F_j^{-1}(q) | X_i > F_i^{-1}(q))$:
>
> | Model | $\lambda^U$ (upper) | $\lambda^L$ (lower) | Asymmetric? |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Gaussian | 0 | 0 | No |
> | Student's $t(\nu)$ | $2t_{\nu+1}\!\left(-\sqrt{(\nu+1)(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)}\right)>0$ | $= \lambda^U$ | No |
> | Clayton$(\theta)$ | 0 | $2^{-1/\theta}$ | Yes (lower tail only) |
> | Gumbel$(\alpha)$ | $2 - 2^{1/\alpha}$ | 0 | Yes (upper tail only) |
> | Frank | 0 | 0 | No |
> | Factor (skew $t$-$t$) | $\tau^U > 0$, analytical formula (Prop 2 in [[Tail Dependence in Factor Copulas]]) | $\tau^L \neq \tau^U$ | Yes |
> | Vine (mixed families) | Depends on pair copulas chosen | Depends on pair copulas chosen | Flexible |
>
> Key finding from Oh & Patton (2012): the Student's $t$ copula is **rejected** for S&P 100 equity returns because it forces $\lambda^U = \lambda^L$; equities exhibit stronger joint crashes than joint booms.
^def-tail-dep-comparison

> [!definition] Exchangeability and heterogeneous dependence
> **Exchangeable copulas** (single-parameter Archimedean) assign the *same* bivariate dependence structure to every pair — clearly inadequate when variables come from different sectors or have different roles.
>
> **Gaussian/$t$**: Not exchangeable (each pair has its own correlation $\rho_{ij}$), but impose the same *type* of dependence (symmetric, elliptical) on all pairs.
>
> **Vine copulas**: Fully flexible — pair $(1,2)$ can be Clayton (lower-tail-dependent), pair $(1,3)$ can be Gumbel (upper-tail-dependent), pair $(2,3)$ can be Gaussian. This is vine copulas' main advantage over factor copulas for moderate $d$.
>
> **Factor copulas**: Flexible *within* the factor structure. The [[Multi-Factor and Block Dependence Structures|block factor model]] (8 industry factors for S&P 100) achieves heterogeneous dependence with only 16 parameters, but all within-block pairs share the same bivariate copula type.
^def-exchangeability

> [!definition] Scalability and the curse of dimensionality
> **Parameter count** ($d$ = number of variables):
>
> | Architecture | Correlation params | Tail/shape params | Total (typical) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Gaussian | $d(d-1)/2$ | 0 | 4950 for $d=100$ |
> | Student's $t$ | $d(d-1)/2 + 1$ | 1 (df) | 4951 for $d=100$ |
> | Archimedean | 0 | 1–2 | 1–2 for any $d$ |
> | D-vine (mixed) | $d(d-1)/2$ copulas × ≈2 params | as above | ≈9900 for $d=100$ |
> | Factor (simple, single) | 0 | 2 ($F_z$) + 2 ($F_\varepsilon$) | 4–6 for any $N$ |
> | Factor (block, 8 sectors) | 8 loadings | 2 × 8 distributions | ≈24 for $N=100$ |
>
> For $d = 100$: vine copulas have ~$10^4$ parameters, making structure selection and estimation difficult even with sequential MLE. Factor copulas scale to $N=100$ trivially because the parameter count is controlled by the number of factors, not by $N$.
>
> **Practical guidance**: 
> - $d \leq 5$: any approach feasible; vine copulas preferred for flexibility
> - $5 < d \leq 20$: vine copulas with truncation (first 1–3 trees)
> - $20 < d \leq 100$: factor copulas strongly preferred; vine copulas need heavy truncation
> - $d > 100$: factor copulas only practical option in current literature
^def-scalability

> [!definition] When to prefer each architecture
>
> **Prefer Gaussian/elliptical when:**
> - Tail dependence is not critical (e.g. macro aggregates, not individual stock returns)
> - You need interpretable linear correlations (easy communication)
> - $d$ is moderate and you have good shrinkage estimators for $\Sigma$
>
> **Prefer Archimedean when:**
> - $d$ is very large and you can accept exchangeability (all variables truly symmetric)
> - Computational simplicity is paramount
> - Used as building blocks inside a vine (each tree-edge copula can be Archimedean)
>
> **Prefer vine copulas when:**
> - $d$ is moderate (up to ~20–30)
> - Different pairs have different tail/dependence behaviour (e.g. mixed asset classes)
> - Sequential data: a natural ordering for D-vine exists (time, geography, credit quality)
> - Software: R (VineCopula), Python (pyvinecopulib)
>
> **Prefer factor copulas when:**
> - $d$ is large (50–500), e.g. equity portfolio, credit basket
> - A common risk factor is economically plausible (market factor for equities)
> - Tail dependence is key but must be parsimoniously parametrised
> - Systemic-risk measures (MES, CoVaR) require portfolio-level simulation
^def-when-to-use

## Examples

> [!example] Architecture comparison on simulated data (Oh & Patton 2012, Table 3)
> **Setup:** $N=10$ variables, $T=500$ observations, true DGP = simple factor copula with $t_4$ factor and $t_8$ errors.
>
> **Comparison** (Kendall's $\tau$ RMSE):
> - True factor copula: best in-sample, best out-of-sample
> - $t$ copula: strong competitor (correctly captures some tail dependence)
> - Grouped-$t$ copula: similar to $t$
> - Clayton/Gumbel (Archimedean): substantially worse (exchangeability fails)
> - Vine copulas: not evaluated (at $N=10$ competitive, but paper focuses on larger $N$)
>
> **Lesson**: When the DGP is a factor copula, the factor model wins. When pairwise structure is heterogeneous, vine copulas have an advantage that grows with the number of distinct pair types.
