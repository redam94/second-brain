# AMSS Natural Migration, Satiation and Lagged-Effect Mechanics

> [!summary]
> The non-media events in AMSS are: a **market-size** event that steers the in-market share to a target $\rho_t$
> (seasonality times trend) with minimal migration; a **de-satiation** event at rate $\lambda$; and four
> **natural-migration** events that pull activity, favorability, loyalty and availability back to an equilibrium. The key
> result is about **carryover**. A natural-migration matrix produces **no lag iff it is row-identical**.
> $Q_\alpha=(1-\alpha)Q_0+\alpha I$ keeps a fraction $\alpha$ of any marketing-induced displacement each period. That is
> a geometric decay with rate $\alpha$, generated by the population dynamics and not imposed on spend.

## Overview

Natural forces set the baseline that marketing builds on (§2.2.1, p.9). When many people are in the market, media has
more people to work on. When satiation is high or the market is small, media-driven activity migrations are capped,
because out-of-market or satiated people must stay inactive (§2.2.3, p.10). Natural migration also decides **how long
marketing effects last**: the stronger the pull back to equilibrium, the shorter the effect (§2.2.4, p.10). This is the
mechanism behind the paper's lagged-effects experiment
([[AMSS MMM Evaluation - Simple Scenario and Lagged-Effects Study]]).

Sets used throughout (App. B, Eqs. 8-9; App. C.1):
$$
\mathcal A=\{s:s_1=\text{in-market}\},\quad \mathcal B=\{s:s_2=\text{unsatiated}\},\quad \mathcal D=\{s:s_4=\text{favorable}\},\quad \mathcal E=\{s:s_5=\text{loyal}\}.
$$

## Main Content

### Event $k=1$: changing market size (§2.2.1; App. B.1)

> [!definition] Market rate and target (§2.2.1, p.9)
> The market rate before event $k$ is $r_{t,k}=n_{t,k,\mathcal A}/n_{t,k,\mathcal S}$. The modeler specifies target
> rates $\rho=(\rho_t)_{1:T}$, factored as $\rho_t=\rho^{(\text{seas})}_t\cdot\rho^{(\text{trend})}_t$. AMSS reaches the
> target **in expectation with minimal migration**:
> - If $r_{t,1}>\rho_t$: in-market people leave with probability $(r_{t,1}-\rho_t)/r_{t,1}$, so
>   $E[r_{t,2}]=r_{t,1}\bigl(1-\tfrac{r_{t,1}-\rho_t}{r_{t,1}}\bigr)=\rho_t$.
> - If $r_{t,1}<\rho_t$: out-of-market people enter with probability $(\rho_t-r_{t,1})/(1-r_{t,1})$, so
>   $E[r_{t,2}]=r_{t,1}+(1-r_{t,1})\tfrac{\rho_t-r_{t,1}}{1-r_{t,1}}=\rho_t$.
> - If $r_{t,1}=\rho_t$: no migration.
^def-amss-market-size

> [!definition] Market entry and exit transition matrices (App. B.1, Eq. 10, pp.32-33)
> **Case 1, $r_{t,1}<\rho_t$ (entry).** The affected population is $a_{t,1,s}=n_{t,1,s}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal S\setminus\mathcal A}(s)$.
> Entrants keep every dimension except market and, if unsatiated, activity. They land in activity state $j$ with a
> modeler-specified probability $\gamma_j$, where $\sum_{j\in S_3}\gamma_j=1$. Satiated entrants must stay inactive. Define
> $$
> h^{(1)}_j(s)=\begin{cases}(\text{in-market},s_2,j,s_4,s_5,s_6)^\top & s\in\mathcal B\\(\text{in-market},s_2,\text{inactive},s_4,s_5,s_6)^\top & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\tag{10}
> $$
> $$
> q^{(t,1)}_{s,s'}=\frac{1-\rho_t}{1-r_{t,1}}\mathbf 1_{\{s\}}(s')+\sum_{j\in S_3}\gamma_j\,\frac{\rho_t-r_{t,1}}{1-r_{t,1}}\,\mathbf 1_{\{h^{(1)}_j(s)\}}(s').
> $$
> **Case 2, $r_{t,1}>\rho_t$ (exit).** The affected population is $a_{t,1,s}=n_{t,1,s}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A}(s)$. Leavers
> also become inactive: $h^{(1)-1}_{s_3}(s)=(\text{out-of-market},s_2,\text{inactive},s_4,s_5,s_6)^\top$, and
> $$
> q^{(t,1)}_{s,s'}=\frac{\rho_t}{r_{t,1}}\mathbf 1_{\{s\}}(s')+\frac{r_{t,1}-\rho_t}{r_{t,1}}\mathbf 1_{\{h^{(1)-1}_{s_3}(s)\}}(s').
> $$
> Migrations are then drawn by Eq. 1 and applied by Eq. 2.
^def-amss-market-matrices

Note that the paper reuses the symbol $\gamma$. The entry distribution over activity states $\gamma_j$ here is unrelated
to competitor strength $\gamma_{t,s}$ in the sales event.

### Event $k=2$: de-satiation (§2.2.2; App. B.2)

> [!definition] De-satiation (§2.2.2, p.10; App. B.2, Eq. 11, pp.33-34)
> Purchasers become satiated in the sales event. At $k=2$ each satiated person de-satiates with probability $\lambda$
> (the **de-satiation rate**). The affected population is $a_{t,2,s}=n_{t,2,s}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal S\setminus\mathcal B}(s)$.
> In-market de-satiators also redraw their activity state from $\gamma_j$, the same distribution as in B.1. With
> $$
> h^{(2)}_j(s)=\begin{cases}(s_1,\text{unsatiated},j,s_4,s_5,s_6)^\top & s\in\mathcal A\\(s_1,\text{unsatiated},\text{inactive},s_4,s_5,s_6)^\top&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\tag{11}
> $$
> the transition probabilities are
> $$
> q^{(t,2)}_{s,s'}=(1-\lambda)\mathbf 1_{\{s\}}(s')+\lambda\sum_{j\in S_3}\gamma_j\mathbf 1_{\{h^{(2)}_j(s)\}}(s').
> $$
> A small $\lambda$ means slow recovery, as with durables and travel. A large $\lambda$ means fast recovery, as with
> perishables. Satiation lets AMSS reproduce **post-promotion sales dips** (p.10).
^def-amss-desatiation

### Event $k=3,\dots,6$: natural migration towards equilibrium (§2.2.4; App. B.3)

> [!definition] Natural migration in dimension $l$ (App. B.3, Eq. 12, p.34)
> For activity ($l=3$), only consumers in $\mathcal A\cap\mathcal B$ (in-market and unsatiated) migrate:
> $a_{t,3,s}=n_{t,3,s}\mathbf 1_{\mathcal A\cap\mathcal B}(s)$. A modeler-specified stochastic matrix
> $\mathcal Q^{(3)}=(\varrho_{i,j})_{S_3\times S_3}$ with $\sum_j\varrho_{i,j}=1$ gives the probability of moving from
> activity state $i$ to $j$. Migration changes **only** dimension $l$. With $h_j(s)=(s_1,s_2,j,s_4,s_5,s_6)^\top$
> (Eq. 12),
> $$
> q^{(t,3)}_{s,s'}=\bigl(1-\varrho_{s_3,s'_3}\bigr)\mathbf 1_{\{s\}}(s')+\varrho_{s_3,s'_3}\mathbf 1_{\{h_{s'_3}(s)\}}(s').
> $$
> Favorability, loyalty and availability work the same way. Only the eligible origin sets differ:
> - **favorability**: only switchers and competitor-loyal consumers ($\mathcal S\setminus\mathcal E$). "Ad exposure won't
>   change the favorability state of an individual who is 'loyal'" (p.34);
> - **loyalty**: only "favorable" consumers can become "loyal" ($\mathcal D$);
> - **availability**: unrestricted.
^def-amss-natural-migration

Each $\mathcal Q^{(l)}$ does two jobs. It fixes the **natural equilibrium** of dimension $l$ (its stationary
distribution) and the **strength of the pull** back towards it, which is the mixing speed.

### Lagged effects as resistance to equilibrium (§2.2.4; App. B.3.1)

> [!theorem] No-lag characterisation (App. B.3.1, Eq. 13, p.35)
> Let $C=\mathcal A\cap\mathcal B$, split by activity into $C_1$ (inactive), $C_2$ (exploratory) and $C_3$ (purchase),
> and let $n=(n_{t,3,C_1},n_{t,3,C_2},n_{t,3,C_3})^\top$. A natural-migration matrix brings **any** distribution back
> to equilibrium $x$ in one step, so marketing-induced changes have **no lag**, iff it is row-identical:
> $$
> \mathcal Q^{(3)\top}n=\|n\|\,x\ \ \forall n\iff\mathcal Q^{(3)}=(x,x,x)^\top .
> $$
> Here $\|n\|$ is the total count (the $\ell_1$ norm). Example (Eq. 13): $Q_0$ with every row equal to $(0.50,0.30,0.20)$
> gives $Q_0^\top n=\|n\|(0.50,0.30,0.20)^\top$.
^thm-amss-no-lag

*Proof sketch.* ($\Leftarrow$) If every row is $x^\top$, then $Q^\top n=\sum_i n_i x=\|n\|x$. ($\Rightarrow$) Apply the
identity to $n=e_i$, which puts everyone in state $i$. Then row $i$ of $\mathcal Q$ equals $x$ for every $i$. The paper
states the equivalence without a proof; this argument fills it in.

> [!theorem] Interpolated lag family (App. B.3.1, Eq. 14, p.35)
> For $\alpha\in[0,1]$ let
> $$
> Q_\alpha=(1-\alpha)Q_0+\alpha I_3.\tag{14}
> $$
> Then for any segmentation $n$,
> $$
> Q_\alpha^\top n=(1-\alpha)\|n\|x+\alpha n .
> $$
> So $\alpha=0$ gives no lag, and $\alpha=1$ ($Q_1=I_3$) gives **permanent** effects, since $Q_1^\top n=n$: the
> intervention's impact "propagates into all subsequent time intervals, until new marketing interventions change the
> distribution once again" (p.35). For $\alpha\in(0,1)$, the deviation from equilibrium satisfies
> $Q_\alpha^\top n-\|n\|x=\alpha(n-\|n\|x)$. A **fraction $\alpha$ of any displacement survives each period**, so after
> $j$ periods without further marketing $\alpha^j$ of it remains. That is exactly the geometric-adstock decay
> $\alpha^j$.
>
> **General rule:** any valid $\mathcal Q^{(3)}$ (entries in $[0,1]$, rows summing to 1) works. Larger diagonal entries
> $\varrho_{i,i}$ mean stronger persistence and longer lag. Larger off-diagonal entries mean faster mixing and weaker
> lag (p.35).
^thm-amss-lag-family

The step from $Q_\alpha^\top n$ to geometric decay uses $\alpha^j$ iteration. That is our derivation from Eq. 14; the
paper does not spell it out. It holds in expectation and ignores multinomial noise and the media events that follow in
the same week.

**Why this matters for MMM validation.** In a regression MMM, carryover is a transform of **spend**: geometric or
delayed adstock ([[Carryover (Adstock) Functional Forms]], [[Carryover Effects and Distributed Lags]]). In AMSS,
carryover is a property of **mindset persistence**. It acts on **people moved**, and how many people are moved depends
non-linearly on reach and frequency. The two coincide only roughly. An adstock MMM fitted to AMSS data with $\alpha>0$
is therefore misspecified in a realistic way, which is what makes the lag experiment informative.

### Post-purchase migration (§2.2.5)

The sales event (the $K$-th) also migrates people. All purchasers become satiated, and favorability and loyalty can
update with satisfaction. Satiation then decays at rate $\lambda$ in later weeks. Formal details are in
[[AMSS Sales Event - Demand Curves, Competition and Post-Purchase Migration#^def-amss-post-purchase]].

### Which dimensions marketing can move (§2.2.3)

> [!definition] Marketing-actionable dimensions (§2.2.3, p.10)
> Marketing can move **activity, favorability, loyalty and availability** ($l=3,\dots,6$). It **cannot** move market or
> satiation ($l=1,2$). People who are out of the market or satiated are stuck in "inactive", so a small market or heavy
> satiation caps the number of activity migrations a channel can drive. How effective an intervention is depends on the
> pre-event segmentation and on the intervention itself, including audience size, frequency and creative efficacy.
^def-amss-actionable

## Examples

> [!example] Lag arithmetic with the Eq. 13 matrix
> **Setup.** Equilibrium $x=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$ over (inactive, exploratory, purchase), with 1,000 eligible people. A TV
> burst moves the distribution to $n=(400,350,250)$, a displacement of $(-100,+50,+50)$ from $\|n\|x=(500,300,200)$.
>
> **$\alpha=0$.** Next week $Q_0^\top n=(500,300,200)$. The 50 extra purchase-state people are gone, so there is no
> carryover.
>
> **$\alpha=0.4$.** $Q_{0.4}^\top n=0.6(500,300,200)+0.4(400,350,250)=(460,320,220)$. The displacement is now
> $(-40,+20,+20)$, which is $0.4$ of the original. The week after, it is $(-16,+8,+8)$, and so on.
>
> **Interpretation.** Raising the diagonal weight produces geometric carryover. The paper uses the same device on
> favorability ($Q^{(4)}_\alpha$) in §4.2.
^ex-amss-lag-arithmetic-eq-13

> [!example] Seasonal market rate in the simple scenario (§4.1.1, Fig. 7, pp.23-24)
> $\rho_t$ is a 52-week sinusoid centred at 0.42 with amplitude 0.175, multiplied by an AR(1) series with mean 1,
> SD 0.1 and autocorrelation 0.3. Fig. 7 shows the market rate oscillating between about 0.25 and 0.70 over the 208
> weeks. The market-size event follows this target, so the pool that media can act on swings by a factor of about 2.5
> within each year.
^ex-amss-seasonal-market-rate-simple

  Stronger mixing means a shorter memory.
